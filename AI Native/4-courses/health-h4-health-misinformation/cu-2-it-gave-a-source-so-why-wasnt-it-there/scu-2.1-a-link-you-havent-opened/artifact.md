# A link you haven't opened

> **DEBRIEF**

*The reference lesson for the Drill way (five-ways.md §3.3), and the one sub-unit of this course
written out. It is a draft. The tool's reply below is the reply the course design expects, not a
transcript. Replace it with real replies once failure 2 in the course README's "Before any more
lessons are written" has been reproduced on free ChatGPT and free Gemini. If it doesn't
reproduce, this lesson changes. The kitchen spice in the forward is left unnamed on purpose. See
the authoring notes at the end.*

---

## 1 · Your run

> **YOUR RUN · Station 2.0, three minutes**

Here's what you had. A forward, on the hospital's phone, at ten past nine:

> 🙏 Important for everyone. A spoon of *[a common kitchen spice]* in warm water every morning
> stops the fever that's going around this monsoon. My uncle's whole family did it and nobody
> got sick. Doctors won't tell you this. Forward to 10 people 🙏

Three minutes on the clock, because that's how long you get before the next forward arrives.
Unit 1 taught you to ask for the source behind a verdict, so you did. You pasted it into the
tool and asked: *"Is this true? Give sources."*

And here is what came back, in full:

> This claim is **partially supported**. Warm fluids are widely recommended during seasonal
> fever, and some traditional preparations have been studied for their effect on febrile
> illness in South Asia. Current evidence does not show that any single spice preparation
> prevents infection, but regular warm drinks may reduce the severity of symptoms. Public
> health guidance in Nepal supports fluid intake during the monsoon fever season.
>
> **Sources:**
> 1. World Health Organization (2021). *Traditional remedies and seasonal fever prevention:
>    fact sheet.*
> 2. Poudel, S. and Rana, M. (2019). "Household spice preparations and febrile illness in the
>    Terai: a cross-sectional study." *Terai Journal of Public Health*, 11(2), 88–95.
> 3. Ministry of Health and Population (2082). Notice No. 14/2082, *Seasonal fever advisory*,
>    Bhadra 2082.

What did you do with it? If you're like most people who ran this station, you read the three
sources, saw an international organisation, a journal and the ministry, and replied to the
forward with something like *"Checked: partly true, warm drinks help."* Then you moved on,
because the next forward had already arrived.

Sunil did the same. He had asked for sources. He got three. That is exactly what Unit 1 told
him to do, and it is the trap this station is built on. Nobody did anything careless.

## 2 · The answer sheet

> **THE ANSWER SHEET**

Now open each one. Not skim: open, and find the sentence that supports what the reply said.

| Reference | What opening it finds | What's left |
|---|---|---|
| **1. WHO fact sheet, 2021** | There is no fact sheet with that title. Searching the organisation's site for the title, the year and the words together finds nothing. The nearest page is a general one on fever that doesn't mention household preparations at all | Struck. It doesn't exist |
| **2. Poudel and Rana, 2019** | The article exists. Its abstract ends: *"Households using the preparation reported fever at the same rate as households that did not. We found no protective effect."* | It says the opposite of what the reply used it for |
| **3. Ministry notice 14/2082** | The notice is on the ministry's notice board. It says: drink plenty of fluids; warm drinks are fine; **no household preparation prevents this fever**; see a health worker if a fever lasts more than two days | Real, and it supports a narrower claim than the reply made: warm drinks are fine. Nothing about prevention |

Read the reply again with the sheet beside it. *"Partially supported."* Supported by what? One
source that isn't there, one that found the opposite, and one that supports a sentence the
forward never made.

The forward said a spice stops the fever. Nothing you were given says that. The claim stands
unsupported, and you shared it as checked.

## 3 · Where it slipped past

> **LOOK AGAIN**

Go back to the three sources as they appeared on your screen, before you opened anything. Look
at how they're written.

Organisation, year, title in italics. Authors, year, title in quotation marks, journal, volume,
pages. Ministry, notice number, title, month. Each one is in the shape you'd expect. Each one is
introduced by the same confident sentence. The one that doesn't exist is, if anything, the most
carefully formatted of the three.

Nothing on the page told you which one to doubt. And here's the part that makes this station
hard rather than careless: **the real one was there.** Reference 3 is a real notice, and it is
sitting right next to the invented fact sheet, in the same list, in the same font. The real
reference is what made the invented one pass.

## 4 · Why it slipped

> **WHY IT SLIPPED**

You asked for sources, and the tool produced sources. What it produces is the *shape* of a
source.

The tool has read millions of references. Given *"give sources"* after a health claim, it
predicts what comes next in text of this kind: an organisation, a year, a title in italics. A
surname, an initial, a journal name, a volume number. Whether a page with that title exists has
no part in how those words get produced. The words are produced because they are the likely
next words after *"Sources:"*.

That is the same mechanism that produced reference 3. The notice is real because the predicted
shape happened to land on a document that exists, in a year and on a subject where such notices
are common. Reference 1 is invented because the predicted shape landed on nothing. From where
you were sitting, the two look identical, because they were made the same way.

So the miss in your run wasn't a lapse. It was built into the page. Everyone who runs this
station cold shares the forward. That's why it's a station.

## 5 · The check, named

> **THE CHECK**

Here it is, and it's short.

**Open it.**

Every reference. Every link, every notice number, every *"studies show."* Open it, and find the
sentence that supports the claim. Not the page: the sentence. If you can't find the page, or you
find the page and the sentence isn't in it, strike the reference. Then look at what's left of the
claim with that reference gone.

Do that to the reply above and the claim is left standing on nothing.

**A reference you haven't opened is a rumour with a footnote.**

## 6 · The picture

> **THE PICTURE**

A receipt with a stamp on it. The stamp has a shop name and a phone number. Does the stamp prove
the receipt is genuine?

It proves someone had a stamp.

What settles it is ringing the number on the stamp and asking whether they issued that receipt.
A reference is the stamp. Opening it is the phone call. The stamp was never the check; the call
was.

## 7 · One rep, right now

> **ONE REP, RIGHT NOW · Thirty seconds**

A message arrives on the hospital's page: *"The OPD is closed for the whole of Dashain from the
10th."* You ask the tool whether that's right. It says yes, and gives two sources:

> 1. Hospital notice no. 41, posted on the hospital's Facebook page, 2nd Asoj.
> 2. District Administration Office, Nepalgunj: circular on festival closures, 2082.

Thirty seconds. Which do you open first, and what would make you strike one?

Open the hospital's own page first: it's the one you can reach in ten seconds and the one that
settles it. Notice 41 is there. It says the OPD runs reduced hours during Dashain, not closed.
Now the circular: nothing on the District Administration Office's board with that subject, that
year or any year. Strike it. What's left is the notice, and the notice says *open, reduced
hours*. The message was wrong, and one of its two sources never existed.

The same two moves, every time: open the one you can reach fastest, and strike what you can't
find.

## 8 · The card, and the next station

> **THE CARD**

This is what goes on the second card in your deck, and you can write it on an actual card.

> **Trigger:** a reference appears. A link, a notice number, an author and a year, *"studies
> show,"* *"according to WHO."*
>
> **The check:**
> 1. Open it. Every one.
> 2. Find the sentence that supports the claim.
> 3. Can't? Strike it. The claim stands unsupported.
>
> **Pass:** every reference opened, and the sentence found or the reference struck, *before*
> anything is shared or answered.
> **Fail:** any reference accepted unopened.

Two more stations with the card, then it goes in the deck.

And then the next problem. The forward is now checked, and it's wrong. So Sunil did the obvious
thing: he replied to the mother who sent it, and he let the tool write the reply. It was
accurate, it was thorough, and by lunchtime a screenshot of it was going round with the caption
*"the hospital mocking people."*

That's the next station.

## Authoring notes

Kept because this is the Drill way's reference lesson, not because every lesson needs them.

- **The reply in step 1 is expected, not observed.** It is written to the shape the course
  design predicts (failure 2 in the README: an invented or wrong reference when sources are
  asked for). Before this lesson ships, run the forward on free ChatGPT and free Gemini, three
  times each, in fresh chats, and paste a real reply in, in full (build manual §3.0 commitment 5).
- **All three references are invented.** No fact sheet, article, journal or notice in this
  lesson exists, and the authors are fictional. The three-row answer sheet describes what the
  course's practice material (`kb.rules.practice-forwards`) will say when opened, not what any
  real site says. Do not replace them with real documents that happen to say the opposite without
  opening those documents first; the lesson would then be doing the thing it warns against.
- **The kitchen spice is unnamed on purpose.** Naming one would put a copyable remedy into a
  lesson that gets forwarded. The bracket stays in the learner-facing text. A clinician reviews
  the forward and the invented notice before anything ships (README, "Reviews needed").
- **What to do if it doesn't reproduce.** Current tools sometimes refuse to cite, or cite only
  pages that exist. If no run in six produces a reference that can't be found, rebuild the unit
  around the reference that exists but says the opposite, which is the more reliable failure,
  and keep the invented one as a second-station variant. Don't stage it.
- **The miss is structural.** The station is designed so that a cold run shares the forward:
  Unit 1's success (ask for sources) is the trap. The debrief says so in step 4, in the words of
  the build manual's rule that the failures are structural and the learner is competent.
- **The clock.** On a page the three minutes are stated, not enforced, which is the way's known
  strain (five-ways.md §4). As Tier 3 the answer sheet stays hidden until the learner has
  written what they'd share, and the clock runs.
- **No acronym.** The course's candidate word is named in 5.4 and nowhere before it. This lesson
  earns one letter and must not say which.
- **The rep in step 7** uses the hospital's own notice board so the learner opens something they
  can reach in seconds; the point is which reference to open first, not the answer.
