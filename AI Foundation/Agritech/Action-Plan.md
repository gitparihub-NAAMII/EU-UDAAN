# AI Foundation Training --- Detailed Action Plan

## Agricultural Agentic AI: From Agricultural Understanding to an Integrated Intelligence System

**Programme:** AI Foundation Training --- Agritech Track\
**Participants:** 40 IT students\
**Duration:** 60 hours\
**Delivery:** 40 hours self-study + 20 hours workshops\
**Project structure:** 10 groups × 4 members\
**Trainers:** 2, each supporting 5 groups\
**Teaching philosophy:** Experience First, Theory Second\
**Learning approach:** Problem-Based Learning (PBL)\
**Analytical framework:** UDAAN U0--U4\
**Final outcome:** One integrated Agricultural Agentic AI prototype
developed collaboratively through a shared GitHub repository.

# PART I --- PROGRAMME DESIGN

## 1. Programme Overview

The programme enables IT students to understand agricultural systems and
develop AI-enabled solutions for real agricultural challenges.

Participants will work in ten groups, with each group investigating one
agricultural problem. Each group will develop four related components,
one per member, and integrate them into a specialised agricultural
intelligence system.

The ten group projects will share a common Agricultural Data Foundation,
common utilities and knowledge resources. Their specialised capabilities
will eventually be connected through a common Agentic AI architecture.

The programme follows four broad phases:

1.  **Understand:** Explore agricultural systems, workflows,
    stakeholders and available data.
2.  **Formulate:** Identify and structure agricultural problems.
3.  **Build:** Develop analytical capabilities, knowledge retrieval and
    specialised agents.
4.  **Transform:** Integrate the capabilities into an AI-enabled
    agricultural workflow and evaluate the prototype.

### 1.2 Expected competencies

By the end of the programme, participants should be able to:

-   Understand agricultural systems, production workflows, stakeholders
    and value chains.
-   Identify agricultural information and data requirements.
-   Explore, acquire and prepare agricultural datasets.
-   Establish an evidence-aware Agricultural Data Foundation.
-   Formulate agricultural problems using the eight elements of Unit.
-   Conduct descriptive, diagnostic and predictive analysis.
-   Organise agricultural knowledge for retrieval.
-   Use local LLMs and RAG for agricultural knowledge applications.
-   Design specialised agricultural agents and tool-using workflows.
-   Coordinate agents through an Agent Manager.
-   Implement validation and human-review mechanisms.
-   Integrate, test and demonstrate an agricultural intelligence
    prototype.

## 3. Teaching Philosophy and Learning Cycle

### 3.1 Experience First, Theory Second

The programme adopts **Experience first, theory second** teaching philosophy:

Instead of beginning with theoretical explanations, each lesson starts
with a realistic agricultural situation or workplace task.

Participants experience a challenge, observe what is happening, discover
the concepts required to understand it, practise the relevant skills and
apply those skills to their group project.

### 3.2 Five-step learning cycle

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Step                    Learning activity       Guiding question
  ----------------------- ----------------------- -----------------------
  1\. Experience          Encounter a realistic   What is happening in
                          agricultural scenario   the real world?

  2\. Observe             Identify the challenge  What do we notice?
                          and information gaps    

  3\. Discover            Introduce concepts,     What do we need to
                          methods and             understand?
                          technologies            

  4\. Practice            Practise the relevant   How can we do it?
                          skills using examples   
                          and tools               

  5\. Apply               Use the skills in the   How can we apply it to
                          assigned group project  our problem?
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

This cycle applies to every module, including agriculture, data
preparation, analytics, RAG and Agentic AI.

### 3.3 Example: introducing agricultural data

The instructor does not begin by teaching CSV, databases, APIs or data
types.

Instead:

1.  **Experience:** Present a cucumber farmer's situation: fruit damage
    has been reported, but the farmer does not have systematic records.
2.  **Observe:** Ask learners what information would help understand the
    situation.
3.  **Discover:** Introduce observations, measurements, records, data
    sources, variables and data quality.
4.  **Practice:** Give learners sample farm records and weather datasets
    to inspect and organise.
5.  **Apply:** Ask each group to identify and prepare the datasets
    required for its agricultural theme.

### 3.4 Teaching-design requirement

Every lesson plan and self-study unit must specify:

-   The real-world scenario or task.
-   The challenge participants will observe.
-   The concepts they will discover.
-   The skills they will practise.
-   The group-project task they will complete.
-   The expected evidence or deliverable.

Theory is introduced when participants need it to understand or perform
the task.

## 5. Module 1 --- Agricultural Understanding and Data Preparation

**Duration:** 10 hours\
**Self-study:** 7 hours\
**Workshop:** 3 hours

**Purpose:** Enable IT students to understand the agricultural systems
behind their assigned problems and establish the initial common
Agricultural Data Foundation.

### 5.1 Learning objectives

Participants should be able to:

-   Explain the agricultural production system relevant to their group.
-   Describe the agricultural workflow and its major activities.
-   Identify the relevant stakeholders and their responsibilities.
-   Understand the information required to operate and manage the
    system.
-   Identify potential agricultural data sources.
-   Distinguish structured, unstructured, visual and time-series data.
-   Explore and prepare initial agricultural datasets.
-   Document data sources, quality, availability and limitations.
-   Contribute to the common Agricultural Data Foundation.

### 5.2 Teaching sequence

**Step 1 --- Experience: encounter agricultural situations**

Present a collection of realistic agricultural situations.

Examples:

-   Farmers report fruit fly damage in cucumber production.
-   Farmers experience difficulty deciding when to irrigate.
-   Farmers are uncertain about soil nutrient requirements.
-   Dairy farmers need to understand changes in milk production.
-   Farmers have difficulty finding reliable market price information.

Each group receives its assigned theme.

Participants discuss:

-   What is happening?
-   Who is involved?
-   What activities take place?
-   What decisions do people make?
-   What information might they need?

Expected output: initial agricultural questions and observations.

**Step 2 --- Observe: investigate agricultural workflows**

Participants investigate the relevant agricultural system.

For example, the Crop Pest Intelligence group explores:

-   Crop production activities.
-   Crop growth stages.
-   Pest identification and monitoring.
-   Crop damage observations.
-   Existing management practices.
-   Farmer and extension officer responsibilities.
-   Information required for pest management decisions.

Other groups undertake equivalent exploration for their assigned themes.

The instructor encourages learners to identify information gaps rather
than assume causes or solutions.

Expected output: preliminary agricultural workflow and stakeholder map.

**Step 3 --- Discover: introduce agricultural concepts and data**

The instructor introduces concepts in response to questions raised
during exploration.

Topics include:

-   Agricultural production systems.
-   Crop and livestock production cycles.
-   Agricultural stakeholders and value chains.
-   Agricultural operations and decision-making.
-   Agricultural observations and measurements.
-   Data sources and data categories.
-   Data collection, quality and provenance.
-   Geographical and environmental information.
-   Agricultural records and documents.

Participants then identify the information needed to understand their
respective agricultural workflows.

Expected output: agricultural data requirement list.

**Step 4 --- Practice: explore and prepare agricultural data**

Participants practise acquiring, inspecting and organising sample
datasets.

Activities:

1.  Explore available public agricultural datasets.
2.  Inspect CSV and Excel files.
3.  Understand variables, units and data types.
4.  Identify missing values and duplicate records.
5.  Check date, location and category consistency.
6.  Examine geographical and weather datasets.
7.  Explore relevant agricultural documents.
8.  Record the source and limitations of each dataset.
9.  Prepare an initial clean dataset where appropriate.

Potential data sources include:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Data category           Potential sources       Intended use
  ----------------------- ----------------------- -----------------------
  Geographical            Open geographical       Location identification
                          datasets,               and mapping
                          administrative          
                          boundaries, OpenCage    

  Weather                 OpenWeather, Open-Meteo Weather forecasts

  Historical climate      NASA POWER, ERA5        Historical
                                                  environmental analysis

  Rainfall                CHIRPS and available    Rainfall analysis
                          national data           

  Agricultural knowledge  NARC, Ministry of       Agricultural knowledge
                          Agriculture, extension  
                          publications            

  Soil                    Soil survey datasets    Soil analysis
                          and laboratory records  

  Market                  Government and market   Price and market
                          information sources     analysis

  Field observations      Surveys, farm records   Local agricultural
                          and field observations  evidence
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Data access, licensing, API restrictions and geographical resolution
must be verified before use. Gridded climate products should not be
treated as precise farm-level measurements.

Expected output: initial prepared datasets and data-source register.

**Step 5 --- Apply: establish the shared Agricultural Data Foundation**

All groups contribute their findings and reusable resources to one
shared foundation.

The shared foundation should include:

-   Common geographical information.
-   Weather and historical climate data.
-   Agricultural and crop information.
-   Soil and environmental datasets.
-   Agricultural documents and knowledge resources.
-   Common data descriptions and metadata.
-   Reusable data acquisition and processing utilities.

Each group also identifies its domain-specific data requirements.

Expected output: initial shared Agricultural Data Foundation and
group-level data inventories.

### 5.3 Three data layers

The common foundation will organise data into three layers.

**Layer 1 --- External and public data**

Public datasets, APIs, government information and institutional sources.

**Layer 2 --- Agricultural knowledge repository**

Research papers, manuals, extension publications, crop calendars,
agricultural guidelines and expert-reviewed documents.

**Layer 3 --- Primary and simulated data**

Farmer surveys, field observations, soil tests, farm records, sensor
data and simulated datasets where real information is unavailable.

Every dataset must be labelled according to its source and whether it is
observed, externally sourced, derived or simulated.

### 5.4 Common data categories

  Category        Examples
  --------------- -----------------------------------------------------------
  Geospatial      Coordinates, elevation, administrative boundaries
  Environmental   Temperature, rainfall, humidity, solar radiation
  Soil            Soil moisture, pH, organic matter, nutrients
  Crop            Crop type, variety, growth stage, planting and harvesting
  Visual          Leaf images, pest images, drone and satellite imagery
  Operational     Irrigation, fertiliser, pesticide and farm records
  Economic        Market prices, supply, demand and transportation
  Knowledge       Research, manuals, guidelines and expert advice

### 5.5 Required deliverables

Each group must produce:

1.  Agricultural System Understanding Document.
2.  Agricultural Workflow Diagram.
3.  Stakeholder Map.
4.  Initial Agricultural Data Requirements.
5.  Data Source Register.
6.  Data Inventory.
7.  Data Quality and Limitation Notes.

The cohort must additionally produce:

8.  Initial Common Data Dictionary.
9.  Shared Agricultural Data Foundation structure.
10. Common data-source and metadata standards.

**Important:** Module 1 establishes initial agricultural understanding
and data availability. It does not formally define the final problem.
Groups refine their data requirements after completing U0.

## 6. Module 2 --- U0: Agricultural Problem Formulation

**Duration:** 6 hours\
**Self-study:** 4 hours\
**Workshop:** 2 hours

**Key question:** What exactly is the agricultural problem, and what
needs to be investigated?

### 6.1 Experience

Present the agricultural situation again, together with the workflow and
information collected in Module 1.

For example:

Farmers cultivating cucumber report fruit fly-related crop damage and
difficulties managing infestation. However, the extent of infestation,
pest species, current management practices and contributing factors have
not yet been systematically established.

Ask participants:

-   What is the actual problem?
-   What should be happening?
-   What is currently happening?
-   What evidence supports the concern?
-   What is still unknown?

### 6.2 Observe

Participants examine the Agricultural System Understanding Document,
workflow, stakeholder map and data inventory.

They identify:

-   What is reported.
-   What is supported by evidence.
-   What is assumed.
-   What is unknown.
-   What information is missing.

### 6.3 Discover

Introduce the eight elements of UDAAN U0:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Element                             Guiding question
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  1\. Context                         Where and under what conditions
                                      does the problem occur?

  2\. Stakeholders                    Who experiences, influences or
                                      addresses the problem?

  3\. Expected Condition              What should happen?

  4\. Observed Condition              What is actually happening?

  5\. Gap and Significance            What is the difference, and why
                                      does it matter?

  6\. Evidence and Uncertainty        What is known, assumed and unknown?

  7\. Scope and Constraints           What are the boundaries and
                                      limitations?

  8\. Formulated Problem              What exactly needs investigation?
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 6.4 Practice

Participants apply the eight elements to their assigned agricultural
theme.

They refine the initial problem, identify investigation questions and
update the data requirements.

### 6.5 Apply

Each group creates its formal U0 problem record and adds it to the
Agricultural Problem Bank.

**Required deliverables:**

-   U0 Problem Formulation Document.
-   Evidence and Uncertainty Register.
-   Investigation Questions.
-   Scope and Constraints.
-   Updated Data Requirements.
-   Agricultural Problem Bank entry.

**Teaching example:**
`AG-U0-001 — Fruit Fly Infestation and Management Challenges in Cucumber Production`.

The example is a teaching scenario. Infestation rates, species, causes
and intervention effectiveness must be established through evidence, not
invented.

## 8. Module 4 --- U1: Descriptive Analytics

**Duration:** 7 hours\
**Self-study:** 5 hours\
**Workshop:** 2 hours

**Key question:** What is happening?

### 8.1 Experience

Present a dataset related to the group's agricultural problem.

For the cucumber example, provide records of fruits inspected and
damaged across ten farms.

Ask participants what they can learn from the dataset.

### 8.2 Observe

Participants inspect the data and identify:

-   Number of farms.
-   Number of observations.
-   Variables and units.
-   Missing values.
-   Differences between farms.
-   Possible patterns.

### 8.3 Discover

Introduce:

-   Data types and variables.
-   Data cleaning and profiling.
-   Frequency and percentage.
-   Mean, median and range.
-   Summary tables.
-   Bar, line and pie charts.
-   Appropriate interpretation of descriptive results.

### 8.4 Practice

Participants use an AI coding agent and structured Markdown instructions
to analyse CSV or Excel files.

Example analytical tasks:

-   Count affected farms.
-   Calculate the proportion of damaged fruits.
-   Compare damage levels between farms.
-   Identify minimum, maximum and average damage.
-   Generate appropriate charts.
-   Prepare a descriptive report.

For the IT track, participants may implement the calculations using
Python, pandas, NumPy and visualisation libraries.

### 8.5 Apply

Each group completes a U1 analysis of its agricultural problem.

**Required deliverables:**

-   Data Description Document.
-   U1 Analysis Requirements.
-   Descriptive tables and charts.
-   U1 Results.
-   Validated Descriptive Analytics Report.

The report must distinguish observations from interpretations and
disclose data limitations.

## 9. Module 5 --- U2: Diagnostic Analytics

**Duration:** 7 hours\
**Self-study:** 5 hours\
**Workshop:** 2 hours

**Key question:** Why might it be happening?

### 9.1 Experience

Present the U1 results and ask participants why the observed patterns
may exist.

For example, some farms show higher fruit damage than others.

### 9.2 Observe

Participants identify differences and potential contributing factors.

For the cucumber example, possible investigation variables include:

-   Temperature and humidity.
-   Rainfall and season.
-   Crop growth stage.
-   Pest monitoring records.
-   Trap counts.
-   Farm management practices.
-   Crop variety.

These are candidate factors, not established causes.

### 9.3 Discover

Introduce:

-   Correlation.
-   Group comparison.
-   Regression.
-   Statistical significance.
-   Relationships between variables.
-   Confounding factors.
-   Association versus causation.
-   Evidence-based interpretation.

### 9.4 Practice

Participants select diagnostic questions, identify relevant variables
and perform suitable analysis.

For example:

-   Is fruit damage associated with trap counts?
-   Does damage differ between crop growth stages?
-   Are there differences between farms with different management
    practices?
-   Are weather conditions associated with observed damage?

### 9.5 Apply

Each group develops its U2 diagnostic report.

**Required deliverables:**

-   Diagnostic Questions.
-   Potential Factors Register.
-   Data Requirements.
-   Analysis Methods.
-   Diagnostic Tables and Visualisations.
-   U2 Diagnostic Analytics Report.

The report must not present statistical association as proof of
causation.

## 10. Module 6 --- U3: Predictive Analytics

**Duration:** 6 hours\
**Self-study:** 4 hours\
**Workshop:** 2 hours

**Key question:** What might happen next?

### 10.1 Experience

Present a situation in which a farmer or agricultural officer needs to
anticipate a future condition.

For example:

A farmer wants to identify which cucumber farms may face higher fruit
fly damage risk during the coming week.

### 10.2 Observe

Participants identify:

-   What needs to be predicted.
-   What historical data is available.
-   What variables might be useful.
-   What prediction period is relevant.
-   What uncertainty exists.

### 10.3 Discover

Introduce:

-   Features and target variables.
-   Training and test data.
-   Regression and classification.
-   Time-series forecasting.
-   Baseline models.
-   Model evaluation.
-   Prediction uncertainty.

### 10.4 Practice

Participants build or inspect a simple predictive prototype where
suitable data is available.

Possible tasks:

-   Predict damage percentage.
-   Classify low, medium and high risk.
-   Estimate future trap counts.
-   Identify seasonal risk patterns.

### 10.5 Apply

Each group prepares its U3 predictive work or documents why prediction
is not justified by the available data.

**Required deliverables:**

-   Prediction Objectives.
-   Prediction Variables Register.
-   Data Requirements.
-   Model Training and Evaluation.
-   Prediction Results.
-   U3 Predictive Analytics Report.

Simulated data may be used for a demonstration, but predictions based on
simulated data must be labelled accordingly.

## 11. Module 7 --- RAG, Local LLM and Agent Basics

**Duration:** 8 hours\
**Self-study:** 6 hours\
**Workshop:** 2 hours

**Purpose:** Introduce knowledge-based intelligence and basic agent
capabilities.

### 11.1 Experience

Present a farmer's question that cannot be answered from numerical data
alone.

Example:

"What management practices should cucumber farmers consider when fruit
fly damage is reported?"

Ask participants what knowledge is required to respond responsibly.

### 11.2 Observe

Participants identify the need for:

-   Agricultural manuals.
-   Research papers.
-   Extension publications.
-   Crop protection guidelines.
-   Relevant local practices.
-   Source verification.

### 11.3 Discover

Introduce:

-   Large Language Models.
-   Local LLMs.
-   Document processing.
-   Text chunking and embeddings.
-   Vector search.
-   Retrieval-Augmented Generation.
-   Prompting and structured outputs.
-   Tools and basic agent workflows.

### 11.4 Practice

Participants:

1.  Organise selected agricultural documents.
2.  Extract and prepare document content.
3.  Build a basic RAG pipeline.
4.  Retrieve relevant information.
5.  Connect retrieved content to a local or accessible LLM.
6.  Generate source-aware responses.
7.  Review the generated output against the source material.

Suggested technologies include Python, FAISS, sentence-transformers,
LangChain or LlamaIndex, and a suitable local LLM such as Gemma or
Llama.

Technology selection should be based on the available hardware and
course environment.

### 11.5 Apply

Each group creates a basic agricultural knowledge retrieval component
and an initial tool-using agent.

**Required deliverables:**

-   Knowledge Source Register.
-   Document Repository.
-   RAG Prototype.
-   Retrieval Test Cases.
-   Basic Agent Specification.
-   Source-aware response examples.

This module introduces the components needed for the later multi-agent
system. It does not require every group to build a complete
orchestration framework independently.

## 12. Module 8 --- Multi-Agent Integration and U4: Decision and Action

**Duration:** 7 hours\
**Self-study:** 5 hours\
**Workshop:** 2 hours

**Key question:** What should be done, and how can the workflow be
improved?

### 12.1 Experience

Present a complete agricultural decision scenario.

For example:

An agricultural extension officer receives a farmer's question about
pest damage. The officer needs to understand the problem, check relevant
environmental conditions, review available pest observations, retrieve
reliable management knowledge and prepare an advisory response.

### 12.2 Observe

Participants identify the separate tasks involved:

-   Understand the question.
-   Identify location and crop.
-   Retrieve relevant data.
-   Analyse the available information.
-   Retrieve agricultural knowledge.
-   Prepare a proposed response.
-   Review and validate the response.

### 12.3 Discover

Introduce:

-   Specialised agents.
-   Agent Manager.
-   Tool calling.
-   Agent communication.
-   Workflow orchestration.
-   Solution generation.
-   Reviewer Agent.
-   Human-in-the-loop validation.
-   U4 Decision and Action.

Distinguish a model from an agent: a model performs a defined analytical
task; an agent selects tools, retrieves information and coordinates
tasks to achieve an objective.

### 12.4 Practice

Participants design and implement a manageable multi-agent workflow.

Proposed architecture:

**Farmer / User → Problem Understanding Agent → Agent Manager →
Specialised Agents → Solution Agent → Reviewer Agent → Human Review
where required → Final Response**

Possible specialised capabilities:

-   Weather and Climate Agent.
-   Agricultural Data Agent.
-   Pest Intelligence Agent.
-   Disease Intelligence Agent.
-   Irrigation Intelligence Agent.
-   Soil and Nutrient Agent.
-   Market Intelligence Agent.
-   Livestock Intelligence Agent.
-   Agricultural Knowledge Retrieval Agent.

Not all agents are called for every question. The Agent Manager selects
only the relevant capabilities.

### 12.5 Apply

Each group integrates its specialised intelligence component with the
common data foundation and agent interfaces.

Groups also identify possible actions, decision requirements,
constraints and monitoring needs under U4.

**Required deliverables:**

-   Agent Architecture Document.
-   Agent Responsibility Specifications.
-   Input and Output Definitions.
-   Tool and API Interfaces.
-   Agent Manager Workflow.
-   Reviewer and Human Review Workflow.
-   U4 Decision and Action Document.
-   Integration Test Cases.

## 13. Module 9 --- Capstone and Evaluation

**Duration:** 6 hours\
**Self-study:** 2 hours\
**Workshop:** 4 hours

### 13.1 Experience

Participants are given realistic agricultural scenarios and must
demonstrate how their integrated system handles them.

### 13.2 Observe

The group examines whether its system:

-   Understands the user question.
-   Selects relevant capabilities.
-   Retrieves suitable information.
-   Uses analytical outputs correctly.

## 15. GitHub Collaboration Workflow

The repository will use a group-level and member-level branching model.

``` text
main
│
├── group-01
│   ├── member-01
│   ├── member-02
│   ├── member-03
│   └── member-04
│
├── group-02
│   └── ...
│
└── group-10
    └── ...
```

Development process:

1.  Trainers create the central repository.
2.  Trainers establish the shared folder structure.
3.  Each group creates its branch from `main`.
4.  Members create individual branches from their group branch.
5.  Members commit work and submit pull requests to the group branch.
6.  The group coordinator reviews and integrates the contributions.
7.  The group submits a pull request to `main`.
8.  Trainers review and merge approved work.

A branch contains the complete repository state, not only the group's
folder. Members should modify their assigned group folder unless
authorised to contribute to shared resources.

No direct commits to `main` are permitted.

## 22. Final Programme Outcome

The final output is not ten independent chatbots or unrelated AI
applications.

It is one integrated Agricultural Agentic AI project with:

-   A shared Agricultural Data Foundation.
-   Common geographical and environmental resources.
-   Agricultural knowledge resources.
-   Ten specialised agricultural intelligence components.
-   Common interfaces and reusable tools.
-   An Agent Manager.
-   A Reviewer and human-validation workflow.
-   A demonstrable and evaluated prototype.

Each group contributes one specialised component, while all groups
contribute to a common foundation and architecture.

**Final implementation principle:**

Start with real agricultural experiences. Help participants observe and
understand the agricultural system. Introduce data and concepts when
they become necessary. Let participants practise the required skills and
apply them to their own agricultural problems. Build the common data
foundation, develop specialised intelligence, and integrate the results
into one Agricultural Agentic AI system.

**Experience first. Theory second. Application throughout.**
